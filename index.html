The Death of the Midrange

How the NBA's shot map changed from 2004 to 2025

In 2004, more than a third of every shot in the NBA was a midrange jumper. Last season it was fewer than 1 in 10. Scroll down and watch it disappear.

2003-04

This is every shot taken in the NBA that season, piled onto one half-court. Each hexagon is a spot on the floor, and bigger hexagons mean more shots per team per game.

See all that pink? That's the midrange: long twos from inside the 3-point line. It was 36% of all shots, more than shots at the rim.

But it was a bad shot

Now the color shows how many points each spot was worth on average. Red is bad, blue is good.

The midrange is almost all red. It averaged just 0.76 points per shot, while a shot at the rim was worth about 1.16 and a corner three about 1.13.

2011-12

Eight years later, not much has changed. Teams still took 30% of their shots from the midrange, even though the numbers said it was the worst shot on the floor.

2014-15: the flip

This is the first season the league took more threes than midrange shots. Watch the blue band around the arc start to thicken while the pink ring thins out.

2018-19

In just four years, the midrange fell from 26% of shots to 15%. The corners, the shortest three on the court, light up on both sides.

2024-25

Today the midrange is under 10% of all shots. Threes are 42%. The pink ring that dominated 2004 is almost gone.

The twist

Here's the strange part: players actually got better at the midrange. It's worth 0.83 points per shot now, up from 0.76.

It still didn't matter. Everything else got better too, and the midrange is still the reddest part of the floor.

Prototype writeup

What we've done so far

We picked a dataset of every NBA shot from 2004 to 2025, about 4.4 million shots, and wrote a Python script that shrinks it down to a shot count, makes, and points for every one-foot spot on the court in every season. We grouped the league's seven shot zones into five (rim, short paint, midrange, corner 3, and above-the-break 3) so the same colors can carry through every chart on the page. We drew a half-court in D3 to real NBA dimensions, including the restricted area, the paint, and the exact 3-point line, and put hexagons on top of it where size shows shots per team per game and color shows either the zone or binned points per shot. The season slider and play button animate the map from 2004 to 2025, labels on the court update with each zone's share, and a linked line chart lets you click any season to jump the court there. Even in this rough version you can watch the midrange ring empty out while the corners and the arc fill in, which is the core of our story: midrange went from about 36% of all shots to under 10%, and threes passed it in 2014-15.

What will be most challenging

The hardest design problem is keeping the court readable while it shows a lot at once, since size and color are already doing two jobs and we still want to add a team filter. We also have to be careful with the data itself, because we found that three seasons (2019-20 through 2021-22) store shot locations at a tenth of the normal scale, and we had to fix them by checking the coordinates against each shot's listed distance. Another challenge is turning these charts into a real story, which means building scroll steps, a "guess the trend" opener, and annotations that point out the surprises, like the fact that rim attempts didn't actually grow and the midrange shots mostly turned into threes. Finally, research we covered in class shows animation is better for presenting than for analysis, so we plan to add small multiples of every season next to the animated court, and fitting both into the page without crowding it will take careful layout work.
